# Woe to the Last Person in the Roundtable

Organizational behavior is such a weird thing. So much of it is positive psychology, but it can be a little dark sometimes. For example, in labor negotiations once an agreement is reached, it is a pretty common understanding that if the union wants to get a yes vote from the membership, they will do it by email or some manual ballot means. But if the union wants the membership to vote no, then they're going to do it in person. Because there is a big difference between meeting in person as a group and meeting over some technology barrier. When people are together in a shared space, emotions can get high, and group dynamics favor dissent. But when people are remote or it's asynchronous in some fashion, and people have time to think and be logical, the dynamics favor consent. This translates pretty well towards whether or not you're doing a meeting or you're using email.

You can argue about the difference between meeting in person or meeting in Teams or whether or not using a Teams chat or an actual email client. There's a bit of a continuum between all of them: in-person meeting, Teams meeting, Teams chat (you monsters), email. But at the extremes, if we take the old saw "this meeting should have been an email", we can also take the reverse to be true, in that the email should have been a meeting. 

When should I call a meeting?

It's shaped like this. Am I just communicating something? Am I just looking for confirmation? Is there anything that's being said here that is contentious or requires opinion or input from the group? If not, if this is just consent, confirmation and affirmation, then that should just be an email straight up. 

When you do send out corporate memoranda, use the bottom-line-up-front (BLUF) approach. Get the meat and potatoes of what you're trying to communicate up front, short and sweet, so people who do scan their email get the most important stuff first and then get the rest of it in the detail later. Kind of like how newspaper columns are written.

But if you want to debate a decision, if you want to pick it apart, find the holes in it, and get genuine feedback, then you're going to do that in a real-time medium, like a face-to-face meeting, or as a second best, you'll do that in a Teams or Zoom meeting. You'll get more robust and vigorous conversation. 

You're more likely to do better in person because on Teams you generally have a bunch of people that are quietly multitasking, cameras off, often nothing but a teeth-filled grid of avatars showing.

In person, you can look around the room and ask who's missing. The old trick is to have an empty chair in the meeting and make the room answer that question: who's not here? You can't do that in a Teams meeting, and you really can't do that over email.

Riffing on that, we should talk about whether or not you're going to have an agenda. It's a contract between you and the participants who have given up their time to attend your meeting in real time instead of an asynchronous email review. Your meeting agenda does need to be more than just a list of bullet points.

If you want to have an effective meeting where you're going to discuss contentious things, then you need to say up front what you're trying to decide, what you're debating. That will stop you from going down rabbit holes. Bounding the meeting by time and saying so up front is also helpful.

> Folks, we have an hour, we have to talk about this really shitty thing. We got the right people in the room, we're going to talk about it for 45 minutes and then we're going to make a decision. Right?

If you have a meeting that opens like that, you're going to have an hour well-spent.

Having kitchen sink kinds of meetings where you have an agenda that's 17,000 different things is not a focused meeting. Some departmental meetings that look like this could be emails, unless you're specifically gathering people to debate something.

So, do people read email? Honestly? No. Not by default. But if the expectation is that the only way they're going to get this information is through reading an email, then they will. FOMO wins.

Gathering people in a room just to receive communication feels good for the people that are delivering the information, but doesn't feel good for the people that are receiving it. It's not a good use of time, and more often than not, there's nothing to refer to after.

Where was I? Agendas. 

It is indeed possible to have an agendaless meeting. If you don't have a clear agenda, that's a good indication that you should run a scrum. I borrowed this from Agile methodologies. Call it an unmeeting if it helps. You start off with a roundtable. You give everybody three questions and two minutes each. Here's one that I use when I want to have a regular standup meeting with my team.

> Hey folks, you each have two minutes. Give me one success. Something that's on your plate right now, and then something that's in your way. Three things: a win, your docket, and rocks that need moving.

One by one, have them answer those three things as succinctly as possible, and then pass it on to the next person. No deep dives, no questions back and forth from the team, no debate. Next person.

Whoever is chairing the meeting, which probably shouldn't be the leader, is capturing the action items and the stuff that requires further conversation.

When the roundtable part of the meeting is over, then and only then do you have your agenda.

Some of those items that come up from the three-question roundtable will be things that can and should be debated in the group because they're meaty things that have urgency to them and that require everybody in the room. But plenty of items don't require everybody to deliberate. Those people involved can go have their own dedicated meeting on that particular item. So, the agenda remains focused and tight.

Many traditional meetings include roundtables at the end after the preordained agenda has been litigated. And often the roundtable is where surprises are revealed and disasters avoided. Woe to the last person in the roundtable chain where everybody's looking at their watch trying to end the meeting and get to lunch.

Roundtable scrum meetings can often be the best use of time. Schedule them to be short, very short, less than a half hour-- 15 minutes if you can get away with it.

SIDEQUEST: Outlook has this feature where you can start a meeting late or end a meeting early when you schedule it. I'd rather start a meeting late so that way folks have an opportunity to just go pee between back-to-back meetings. Pro tip from the Pandemic.

Anyhoo.

Before you send the next meeting invite, you should ask yourself some key questions. What am I looking for out of this meeting? Am I looking for agreement or am I looking for disagreement? What medium serves that? Is it Outlook? Is it Teams? Is it a chat? Is it a face-to-face meeting? What is the decision? Who owns it? Who's missing? How does it end? When does it end?

If you can't answer those questions, send the damn email.
