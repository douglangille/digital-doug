# Self-ware

I want to tell you a story. 

This was decades ago and I can't really remember the details, but I was big into the original DOOM game when it was shareware. I played that thing a lot and spent too much time tying up the phone line with my modem on the local BBS swapping WAD files. I was never skilled enough to make my own.

In the early '90s, shareware games like DOOM were quite popular. Before the internet, you'd download games and utilities and anything really off the local bulletin boards. Not all of it was above board. And I even sent some of the developers a buck or two. I was in my twenties and broke. 

Sometimes you'd get a diskette in the back of a magazine. These disks would be stuffed full of games, utilities and shareware. Some worked okay, a lot of it was junky demos, nagware and the like. There were also gems.

These days it's all about app stores and subscription services, which has turned everything into a bit of a cash grab mess. Shareware that you might have paid ten dollars for became something you pay for every month. Often it's a small utility, and you're paying for the convenience. Of course, the App Store gets its cut.

And now hordes of unscrupulous solo developers vibe code shitty applications for the app store and charge a monthly subscription. It has given me a sour taste in my mouth.

Why should I pay fifteen bucks a month for an app that tracks my weights at the gym? It's useful, but it's not worth fifteen dollars a month. Or three dollars a month for a widget that adds a fancy pants weather box to my iPhone home screen. I might pay once for something like that, but a subscription? Come on boys and girls, that's ridiculous.

Here we are in the age of AI. These things are so good at coding that you can speak an application into existence.

You can make your own self-ware. Just don't turn it into a get rich quick scheme.

I've vibe coded a few web experiences, my own blog templates, some agentic skills and scripts, and some single page infographics-- basically things I've also done by hand over the years. Coding is something I've always done. I even vibe coded a virtual escape room as a proof of concept. But the first thing I vibe coded that resembled bona fide software was my Blue Pencil Marginalia plugin for Obsidian.

I could have written Blue Pencil by hand. I have the skills and can learn the tooling, but possess neither the time nor energy. What I had was a need and an idea. Vibe coding was the toolkit I employed to make it real and something useful for me. 

That ain't nothing and it speaks to the future of software. I mean, look, no one codes in assembly language and most people don't code in C. Programming languages mature and capabilities produce emergent properties of the tools. Development moves up the stack as tools abstract the lower levels. Coding agents are another level of abstraction that may hallucinate or otherwise screw up. There are certainly more questions than answers.

Anyway, my Blue Pencil plugin is a copy editor tool I made for myself. I'm not selling it and I may not even put it out there for free. It works inside Obsidian, where I do my writing, and it fits my own writing process.

It allows me to take a piece of my writing and highlight spelling and grammar with the red and green squigglies we all love/hate. But I also have it pattern match my writing with a blue highlighter. I originally had it looking for AI tells in my hybrid writing, but to be honest, I don't care about the AI patterns and pretty much all the writing is my own anyway. What I do care more about is whether or not the writing's good. I do get it to highlight in my text where I have the patterned sentence constructions, dangling which clauses, groups of three, long/short/long/short sentence patterns, and run-on sentences like this one. Doesn't matter if it's AI generated or Doug crafted, bad writing is bad writing. And that's why I conjured up a digital copy editor holding a blue pencil.

It does most of the work mechanically with rules. It uses Harper for the grammar and an open source spell checker engine. I wove in the pattern matcher. Everything is marked up on the page where I can eyeball it to see what I want to change and what I'm fine with it. 

Part of the original reason for Blue Pencil was about mitigating my own AI use, but it turns out those reviled AI patterns were always in my writing when I scanned a sampling of my pre-AI writing.

I find it funny and oddly satisfying to see my writing scribbled blue. My first drafts are almost entirely dictated and grafted together like a ransom note. And my speech is apparently highly patterned even more than my direct writing. Do I sound like a robot? Jeepers. Anyway, I know full well when AI writes something and when spoke-wrote it myself. It just cracks me up that I can see my own human patterned use too. And, importantly, do something about it.

I have over 30 years experience writing publicly. I know that the Blue Pencil mechanical checks do not account for nuance. It's the same with AI detectors. That is, not every "it's not x, it's y" sentence construction is AI generated. I built Blue Pencil with the understanding that it would reveal signals and patterns, not edicts and condemnation. So how do I know it's right? I don't. That's the point. I'm the human that gets to check.

The whole point of this tangent is that I needed a plugin for Obsidian that didn't exist in a way that I needed it to. Other plugins were there, but they imposed a writing process and methodology that was not compatible with my own. So I worked with an agent and built a thing for a user base of one: me.

No one else can use this thing but me because it's made for me and my process. This is why vibe coding self-ware makes sense. You're making it for you. You're making it to work the way that you want it, the way that you need it to work with the tools that you use. Dazzit.

Creating your own tools is a satisfying form of craftsmanship. 

You can bring craft to the AI table. In fact, you must.

There's a dark side and a cost. When you're making an app for yourself, there isn't a "done" moment. Unless you're disciplined to follow software development best-practices and not skip a few steps here and there, you'll always be adding features and tweaking this and that. Before you know it, you'll be another Homer Simpson designing the perfect car.

Or worse, you'll spend more time perfecting your tools instead of using them.

Sometimes the right answer is to pay the damn subscription fee and get on with it.
