# Is There Any There, There?

I finally got around to reading the federal government's national AI strategy: [AI for All](https://ised-isde.canada.ca/site/ised/en/canadas-national-artificial-intelligence-strategy-ai-all). They unleashed it in June 2026. There's a lot of buzzy announce-y things there, but buried in the fluff is a meaty promise:

> "Canada will ensure all post-secondary students have access to trusted AI agents, putting capable, personal AI tools in the hands of the next generation of Canadian workers, researchers, and innovators."

Some of those words are doing a lot of work. 

So I have questions.

First is: who is going to provide such a thing? Because, to be honest, there aren't that many players, if you want something that's sovereign and trusted, and agentic, and available to every higher ed learner in the country.

## What comes after?

If people are building stuff with agent tools, they're building these elaborate contraptions. I should know. I've built them myself. So when you no longer have access to the tool, i.e. you graduate, what happens? 

`crickets`

I have this personal AI second brain called Forge that I've done my very best to make vendor agnostic. It sits in GitHub. It is largely run through Claude Code, but I do run other agents against it. But if I were to lose Claude Code, I wouldn't necessarily be able to switch tools one-for-one immediately. There would be hiccups. I can get OpenCode and Augure to work. I can get Mistral Vibe to work. 

But I did bake in some dependencies. 

I can deal with that as a reasonably AI literate nerd. Everyone else tho...

It's a house of cards. And I think that I've probably built one better than most. But most people, especially normies who don't have an IT background, who have built these things, have built a more fragile system than they're even aware of.

And worse, many haven't found a way to use the tools safely and be policy compliant. Shadow AI use is a real thing.

## What's the point?

If the skill is to provide our learners a digital literacy and an AI literacy framework, and we go and choose a particular tool because of politics and nationalism, i.e. something sovereign in Canada, what does that actually do to the market?

At the community college level, we don't teach digital image manipulation. We teach Photoshop. Photoshop is the skill that shows on your resume that helps a student get employed, not digital image manipulation. 

And I think this plays out in spades when we talk about AI. The expectation isn't that I learned how to use Cohere's or Augure's random sovereign trusted tool, or that I can do agentic AI. It will be: I know how to use Claude, or I know how to use ChatGPT. It'll be something very, very specific. It'll be about the tool.

The product name becomes the technology. Band-Aids and Kleenex are great examples. So is Google. Many people talk about ChatGPT as synonymous with AI chatbots just in general, and I think Claude has the same kind of street cred. Nobody really talks about Gemini and Grok and whatever quite the same way. 

Hell, even framing AI as a skill is a bit misleading (a topic for another day).

Anyway, when you go and look at a resume, you're not looking for any niche tools. You're looking: can they use either one of those two big ones? I know I'm going to be in the market looking for an AI analyst, and I'm going to be looking for key things. I'm going to be looking at somebody who can actually make the frontier tools work. Having the training, the quick and dirty education, is one thing, but actually building stuff is the real demonstrable skill. I know that. But the average employer doesn't. The average employer does not know how to evaluate what people build. They only know how to evaluate a stack of resumes based on keyword screening: do they have the credential, are they ticking the boxes?

## Ages like milk

This stuff is going to age like milk. The opportunity is right here and right now, and then it's going to be gone. There was a time when not every learner had access to the internet. Now the internet is assumed. The market commoditized internet access, and I think that's going to happen with AI tools. They are going to be commoditized too, and we won't be doing these kind of things.

It's funny, this doesn't actually show up with students. Not at first, anyway. It shows up with staff. When I first started at the NSCC last century, having internet access wasn't really assumed. For some of our staff that actually required it, we stood up a dial-up RAS (remote access server) for people to be able to get online without a financial burden through our fractional T1. It was an equity of access to a trusted tool thing. Sound familiar? We ran that for a couple years. Then, as broadband internet became more affordable, people started switching to it, and all of a sudden we were down to like two or three users, and we quietly turned it off. It was like boiling a frog. There was no hard switch. The solution that we built was only good for a hot minute.

We built this huge Polycom infrastructure, built these Polycom video conferencing rooms and all of that jazz, and then Zoom and Microsoft Teams came along and obviated almost the entire shooting match. Yes, we still have some Teams rooms and such. But all of this infrastructure for Polycom equipment was just a point in time expense.

I could continue to flagellate a skeletonized equine specimen by reminding folks of laptops and tablets, but I'm sure you, Dear Reader, can come up with your own examples of technology adoption curves quite readily.

When you look at this emerging technology piece, you really have to think about when to get on and when to get off. If you invest heavy at the wrong time, you're stuck looking at a bunch of old gear kicking around. Commoditizing of technology can happen in an incredibly short window. 

What's the clock on it? I have no idea. Is it six months, one year, two years, five years, ten years? Who the blazes knows? You can be sure of this one: somebody's going to make money off of it, and if somebody's going to make money off of it, it has to be turned into a product. So if we are too slow in building these kind of things, as a country and as a set of higher education institutions, if we don't get this right in time, we will find that the market will have eclipsed us, and we won't have an option of building a sovereign AI tool for every learner in the country, because the market will have said: everybody use Claude. The flip side is that we move too quick and the market moves anyway, and then we're stuck with a bunch of investment that we can't get any return on.

## The asterisks

If the Feds came to us and said, hey, everybody is going to have to give all of the students across the country access to something on the Cohere framework, then I guess that's what we would do. But that doesn't change the fact that we, all of us higher education institutions, are either in bed with Microsoft or Google. And it's fine to give students access to stuff, but what are you going to do for your staff and faculty? Ideally you would give them access to the same tools that you're giving for students. But the "AI for All" strategy doesn't talk about staff and faculty. Not all of them. Not really.

With respect to Copilot and our Microsoft 365 license: the max license that many institutions can afford is A5, which only provides access to the web-grounded chatbot-level tool. To actually use some of the agentic tools, it requires an expensive upgrade to the license. Microsoft has made that hard to even actually articulate, because they separate between this Copilot for Office product (somewhere north of $440 CDN per user annually). This would give you a co-work kind of environment. And then they have this Copilot Studio environment, which is the true agentic piece, that bills on a consumption basis. So none of it's one-for-one, or like for like. It's actually quite hard. 

One idea is, maybe we find a way to get a discount with the frontier models, with the Googles and the Microsofts and ChatGPTs. I know part of this push is to move to a more sovereign solution. But what if we don't have sovereign tools at scale? Even looking at Cohere, Cohere's funding partners aren't exactly pure Canadian either. There's a lot of American investment in those things. I don't think it's as clean as everybody wants it to be. Just because the data centres are physically hosted in Canada doesn't mean that it's actually a truly Canadian thing. And it could be just as likely that we buddy up with one of the EU's monstrosities, like Mistral.

There's no clean answer, because the questions are still so nebulous. Some are questions of scale, some are policy stewardship, and a whole lot of question about definitions.

I'm not sure I could sign my name on the dotted line just yet.

What does sovereign mean? What does agentic actually mean?
