# Make Something Beautiful

Halloween is one of my favorite holidays, and this week it's on Saturday. The clocks go back that same night, so for a November 1st All Saints Day of all things, we get our time change. Time change has been weird across the country this year, because a couple of jurisdictions don't bother with the change at all anymore.

But regardless. It's getting darker. Holy smoke, it is dark by six o'clock pretty much now, and after the time change it'll be dark by five. A little more daylight for a little while. Fine. Then it gets dark earlier again.

This is usually the time of year I re-litigate the whole dark mode versus light mode thing. Not this time. I settled on dark mode a few months ago. I wasn't going to keep doing the light-mode-during-the-day, dark-mode-at-sunset, Night Shift, True Tone rigmarole. I just got tired of the shift, the jarring change between light and dark on something I look at a lot. And now that it's dark most of the time anyway, dark mode is just the natural state. Easier on my eyes. Not necessarily easier on anybody else's. That's all that counts, really. My own preference.

Mostly I've configured things dark mode where I can. And to be honest, most of the time dark mode is perfectly fine, especially when I'm on a Mac and I have a camera in front of me. The darker background doesn't shine off on my glasses, and it's really practical.

The whole business about whether or not it changes eye strain or reading comprehension, I think most of that is fractions of a percentage difference. There might technically be a difference. In practical terms, there isn't. There are other things that are impacting your ability to concentrate or cause eye strain. Blink, god damn it. Or maybe take a break. And don't do things tired. But whether or not it's dark or light mode is not going to be the productivity boon you think it is.

This is also the time of year you start feeling the lack of vitamin D. Welcome to Canada. I like to find the joy anywhere I can, and one of the joys I find is making sure my computing experience is consistent and pleasing to be in, because I'm at a screen a good chunk of the day. One of three screens, really. You get up and stare at a six-inch screen, spend the working day on a fifteen-inch screen, spend the evening on a nine-inch screen or a fifty-five-inch screen depending on whether it's the iPad or the big TV. A lot of screens. In a way, to measure your life, too. Pretty sad.

So that's the whole point: having an aesthetic experience when you're looking at screens a lot of the time, and it's dark most of the time, kind of matters to me.

I think your computing environment can be aesthetic. Using a computer, particularly for a long amount of time, or switching between devices, can feel like a continuity of experience. And that's what you get when things are the same. You're moving from one version of your experience to another version of your experience. It's yours. It's your safe zone, if you will.

I actually hyper focused and put [Rosé Pine](https://rosepinetheme.com) everywhere I could. I have Rosé Pine on my iPhone and iPad, set up in my desktop wallpaper, set up on my Mac. I have all of my apps, my terminal apps in particular, configured to a Rosé Pine theme, and my Obsidian and VS Code themes.

The places where I specifically didn't do it were where the operating system and the Mac really made it impossible to do so. Customizing the Mac interface for Rosé Pine is almost impossible to actually get right. You only really have the wallpaper and the accent color to work with, so I left the accent color the classic operating system blue and just worked on the wallpaper. The Microsoft apps are pretty much the same way. A big bag of Skittles. You don't have a lot of customization with it. Yeah, you can change the accent color to be a purple or a pink, but you can't actually truly get it to a Rosé Pine palette, so it just looks weird, so I left that full on Microsoft blue. Funny enough, it actually matches the Apple operating system color. But everything else I did my best to tint Rosé Pine.

I did lean on the warmer side of the palette, because the palette does have a Rose Love side and then it also has a Pine Foam side, so I do tend to lean on the Rose Love edge.

I think when you come across an interface element that doesn't match, it really takes you out of it, and it's jarring, and it does give you a moment to think about whether or not, well, you're not in the moment anymore. You're focusing on whatever interface element that comes up, and I think that's not good usability. I'm really irritated at Apple and Microsoft for not providing that level of customization with their tools.

It feels like a swing and a miss, because it just hurts my brain and I can't really pinpoint why it does, but I just identify strongly with the continuity of experience. It's like muscle memory in a way, right? You expect when you right-click something, you're going to get a menu. When you double-click on something, an action is going to happen. When you see a particular color on the screen, it indicates this, that, or the other. When you see a bold font, it means it's important. When you see that it's italic, it means that there's a different kind of emphasis on it. Underline probably means a link. You know what I mean. So I think color has an aesthetic piece, an expectation piece, and a muscle memory piece.

I've tried other palettes in the past. One of my favorites that I've used was the Solarized light dark palette, and I still do enjoy that a lot, but it didn't really have a whole lot of character. I guess that's largely the point, that it's supposed to disappear into the background.

As far as why I prefer Rosé Pine right now, I think it's more about the muted nature of it than anything else. I don't really like yellow, if I'm honest. I like the reds, greens, and blues. Browns and oranges, sure. But, taste is ephemeral. What I like today will be different than what I like tomorrow.

Anyway.

In case you haven't caught this, I can really take things too far. And I aimed my laser focus on applying my beloved color palette to my two websites as their design language: Digital Doug and [Darkling Whim](https://douglangille.ca) both have a similar construction. In fact, they're essentially the same bespoke theme that Claude and I developed together, and they both use Rosé Pine as their color palette, with minor accessibility tweaks, but in different registers. Darkling Whim leans a lot more on the Rose Love side, whereas the Digital Doug leans more on the Pine Foam side. And of course, I came up with typography for both sites that match. We can nerd out on fonts another day.

That accessibility tweak is a real number, by the way: stock Rosé Pine's light-mode love accent measures out at 3.8 to 1 contrast, too low to read comfortably as text. So I deepened it myself. With help from the robots, naturally.

Similarly, the "self-ware" apps I vibe-code also feature a Rosé Pine palette. An RSS reader that connects straight to my Obsidian vault through another app I crafted to host my notes the way I like. I spend a lot of time in my notes. I didn't want my tools to look like Claude-slop orange and brown or ChatGPT blue and purple. I want my tools to look like I'd taken the time and care to actually build them. So I picked a color scheme and went with it.

I don't have any particular advice here. I can only offer what works for me. That it was worth it for me to take the time and to think about my work environment and how I wanted to spend way too many hours a day in front of these shiny screens. And because these things matter to me, I wanted to take the time and do it right. It wasn't a crazy lot of time. You can go to [Coolors](https://coolors.co) or any of these palette sites out there, you can find palettes that exist and look at them and say, okay, this one speaks to me, this one doesn't. You can just Google up something too. Or you can even make your own. God help us, there was the hot dog theme in Windows 3.1, and that attracted some folks that really liked to have that level of contrast. There's all kinds of reasons to choose a color palette. No judgement here. Probably.

I think for it to matter, it has to serve a few things. One, it has to mean something, as in there has to be some consistency of experience, that certain colors mean certain things. It has to be something you can comfortably look at.

There's another reason to look at this as well, and this is a bit of an AI spin. There is this AI slop that you see out there that's based on the design language defaults that these things actually put out. For example, ChatGPT and Gemini love blue and purple and Claude favours orange and brown. Folks can pick out AI slop a mile away.

There is something to be said for picking a design language that you actually like, and which I did. So, Claude actually has these skills for building design languages, and I think it's worth doing it for a couple reasons. One, so that way your work doesn't look like everybody else's work and it's not AI slop. Two, that if your organization does have a design brand language, that when you do generative outputs or assisted outputs from a large language model, that you can actually give it the design language kit. That way an output that you generate from these things actually looks like your employer's sanctioned kit. And then there's what you'll produce for yourself or to share with others. If you don't want it to look like a robot made it, and you want the fact that you put a lot of effort and thought and care into the work you did, a sense of craft, then you want the design language to reflect that. Don't let default design cheapen your work.

One of the easiest ways to make an impact on your design is with a color palette, and you don't have to build it from scratch. You can research what's already out there. I landed on Rosé Pine because it was really attractive to me, and it was well-designed, well-thought-out, and I could have enough variation within it. That's me though.

One of my values is craftsmanship, making beautiful things. That includes my own working environment.

I made my own workbench during COVID with upcycled materials. Cleaned it up, took the care to make it look the way I wanted, hand-rubbed it with linseed oil so it'd be a beautiful place to work. Ironically, with a lot more yellow than I care for. In my computing environment, I want that digital workbench to be just as pleasing a place to work.

Make something beautiful. Especially in the cold dark of winter that's coming, when the palette outside is monochrome and dark and just smaller somehow. Christmas notwithstanding. That's probably reason enough to go take the time, pick a color scheme, and be intentional about it. Own it.
